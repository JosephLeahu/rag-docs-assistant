# Lahars (Volcanic Mudflows)

## What is a lahar
A lahar is a fast-moving slurry of water, rock, and volcanic debris that flows down river valleys.
Lahars can be triggered by eruptions that melt snow and glacier ice, by heavy rain on loose volcanic
deposits, or by the collapse of weakened volcanic rock. They can travel tens of kilometers and move faster
than people can run.

## Mount Rainier
Mount Rainier is considered the Cascade volcano with the greatest lahar risk because of its large volume of
glacier ice and hydrothermally altered rock, and because many communities in the Puyallup and Carbon River
valleys are built on old lahar deposits. The Osceola Mudflow, about 5,600 years ago, reached the area now
occupied by communities near Puget Sound. The Electron Mudflow, roughly 500 years ago, reached the
Puyallup River valley.

## Detection and warning
A lahar detection system on Mount Rainier, run by the USGS with Pierce County Emergency Management, uses
instruments in river valleys to detect ground vibrations from approaching lahars and trigger warnings.
Residents are taught to move to high ground rather than try to outrun a lahar along a valley floor.

## A historical lesson
In 1985, a relatively small eruption of Nevado del Ruiz in Colombia melted summit ice and generated lahars
that buried the town of Armero, killing about 23,000 people. The disaster is widely cited as the reason
lahar warning systems matter.
